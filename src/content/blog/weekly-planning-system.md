Let me be upfront about who this post is for. If your life is already sorted — nothing left you're chasing, nothing you still want to build — this isn't for you. Go enjoy that, genuinely. I'm not there. I don't expect to be there for a long while, and I'm not sure "arriving" is really how this works anyway. This is a system for people still climbing. If that's you, here's the process I run every week to make sure the climbing actually goes somewhere.

## Step 1: Vision

You only need to revisit this step occasionally, but you can't skip it. Before you can plan a week, you need a working draft of what your entire life is supposed to add up to.

The exercise I've found most useful: imagine your own funeral. Who's there. What they say about you in the eulogy. If that's too morbid for you, picture yourself at 70 instead, looking back. Either way, the point is the same — you're trying to work backward from an ending to figure out what actually matters to you, as opposed to what you've just absorbed from everyone around you.

Don't spend forever on this. A working draft is enough. The point isn't to produce a finished document — it's to give yourself a decision-making framework you can use in every step after this one. You'll revise it as you go. That's fine, expected even. Just make sure each revision *builds on* the draft rather than throwing it out, because a vision you rewrite from scratch every few months isn't a vision, it's just mood.

## Step 2: Goals

Once you've got a vision, you can start deriving actual goals from it — and this has to happen in layers, from vague to brutally specific.

**Long-term goals** come straight from the vision and are allowed to be a little fuzzy. Something like "retire with a million dollars by 40."

**Near-term goals** get more concrete. "Get the track record that makes retiring at 40 with a million dollars realistic" — say, working toward a leadership role at a company that's actually going somewhere.

**Now-term goals** are where the vagueness has to stop completely. These are what you're actually going to do, starting now, broken all the way down. "I'm putting in full-time hours this year, I'm over-delivering on every project my team ships, and I'm getting the certification my manager says the next promotion requires." No hiding behind ambiguity at this level — if a goal here isn't specific enough that you'd know immediately whether you hit it or missed it, it's not a now-term goal yet.

## Step 3: Pick and Choose

Here's the hard part. From your list of now-term goals, you pick two or three. Not five, not "as many as I can handle." Two or three.

This is genuinely difficult, because everything on that list will feel equally urgent and equally important — that's usually why it made the list in the first place. But nobody actually juggles more than two or three serious pursuits at once. Anyone who tells you otherwise is either lying or spread so thin that none of it is actually moving.

Take real time deciding. Then, once you've decided, commit completely and stop relitigating it. This is the part people get wrong the most — the moment things get hard, it's incredibly easy to convince yourself the original decision was flawed, that you didn't have enough information, that you should really be doing one of the other seventeen things instead. That's not new information showing up. That's just weakness looking for a rationalization. Don't fall for it. Finish what you started before you let yourself pick something new.

## Step 4: The Weekly Grind

This is the part that actually runs every single week — same time, every time. Sunday evening works well for most people. The entire point of this step is to remove thinking from your weekdays. You've already decided what matters; now you're just scheduling it, so that when Monday hits, you're executing instead of deliberating.

Here's the run of it:

1. Review the vision.
2. Quickly retrace the logic — how your long-term goals produced your near-term goals, which produced the two or three now-term goals you're currently working.
3. List what you need to do this week to move those now-term goals forward, and rough out how long each thing will take.
4. List the miscellaneous stuff — appointments, errands, whatever else has a fixed time attached to it.
5. Pen in anything from step 4 you don't control — appointments, classes, whatever's already fixed.
6. Pencil in everything from step 3, scheduled into the gaps. This is where the sacrifice happens, because you will always want to schedule more than you can actually do.

## A Few Rules I've Learned the Hard Way

- **Don't schedule everything.** Routine stuff — classes, laundry — doesn't need a slot. Writing it down just adds stress for no benefit.
- **Don't schedule back to back.** If you block two hours for linear algebra and one hour for something else right after, you're lying to yourself. It won't happen that cleanly. Leave gaps — real ones — to absorb your bad time estimates and the random stuff that always shows up (your mom calling to ask a favor, whatever). The tradeoff is real: more gaps means less gets scheduled. You'll only find the right balance after a few weeks of getting it wrong.
- **The system doesn't work without the underlying habits.** Sleep. Exercise. Don't drink on weekdays — save it for Friday or Saturday. Don't stay up past 12:30 on a weeknight. Do things because you decided they're worth doing, not because they feel good in the moment.
- **Ten minutes every morning, right after your shower, sit somewhere quiet and review the vision.** This is not optional and it's not filler — it's genuinely the most important ten minutes of your day. You need to know *why* you're doing what you've scheduled and actually feel something about it, or the schedule is just a list of chores.
- **Every Sunday, before you plan the next week, look back honestly at how the last one went.** This step requires actual willpower, because it's tempting to skip the reckoning and just move on to planning something new.

One last thing: this system is not fixed. It's going to evolve for you over weeks, months, years, the way any real system does when a real person is actually using it. Take it, run it, and change what doesn't fit. The version I'm running today isn't the version I started with, and it won't be the version I'm running in a year.
