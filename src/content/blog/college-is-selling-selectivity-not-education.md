What's the purpose of education? Depending on who you ask, it builds a person's character and morality. Or it cultivates reason, social cohesion, and the transmission of a culture from one generation to the next. It fosters civic engagement. It's the great engine of economic mobility.

Let's dispense with all of that. Higher education in the developed world isn't reliably delivering any of it. What it's actually doing is something much narrower, and much more useful to understand: it's filtering candidates for the job market.

## Why governments subsidize a filter

Here's the thing that made college subsidies look like an obviously good idea for decades: studies kept showing that college graduates earn more over a lifetime than people who don't go. Governments looked at that correlation and drew the natural conclusion — get more people degrees, get more people into the higher-earning bucket. Simple.

Except it doesn't work that way, because the value of a college degree was never really about what you learned. It was about how few other people had one. The moment everyone has a college degree, having one stops telling an employer anything. You've subsidized the credential into a commodity and destroyed the very signal you were trying to spread around.

## Start with the labor market, not the classroom

If you want to understand what higher education actually does, don't start with curricula. Start with the labor market it feeds.

If there's a path to an aspirational, upwardly mobile living that doesn't require a degree, the degree is worthless to the people who find that path. Mark Zuckerberg and Bill Gates are the most famous examples, but they're not the real proof — the real proof is the millions of electricians, welders, plumbers, and HVAC techs who went straight from high school into an apprenticeship and are now earning more than plenty of English majors, without six figures of debt attached.

So who actually needs the degree? Corporations hiring for white-collar roles, and licensed professions like medicine and law, that need a way to filter recruits before investing in training them.

And here's the part almost nobody says out loud: nothing you learn in a four-year undergraduate program actually prepares you for the job. Doctors, lawyers, investment bankers, consultants, marketers — all of it is still an apprenticeship in practice. You learn by doing, under someone who already knows how, on the job. The degree gets you in the door. It doesn't teach you the work.

## The product is undifferentiated. The gate isn't.

A math degree from a mid-tier state school covers the same material as a math degree from MIT. Same textbooks, mostly the same theorems, often taught by professors trained at the same handful of graduate programs. The educational product itself is not meaningfully different.

What's different is who got let in. Selective schools aren't selling a better education — they're selling the fact that they already filtered eighteen-year-olds down to the ones corporations want to talk to. The degree from a selective school isn't proof you learned more. It's proof you cleared a bar before you showed up.

That distinction used to be easy to miss, because "getting an education" and "getting filtered" happened in the same building, on the same four-year schedule, for the same price. They're not the same thing anymore — if they ever really were — and the gap is only getting more obvious now that the entirety of human knowledge is sitting on the internet for free, explainable on demand by an AI that doesn't care what your GPA is. If the school were actually selling the education, that alone should have collapsed its price by now. It hasn't, because the education was never the product. The selectivity was.

## Evaluate it like an investment, because it is one

This has a very practical consequence for anyone eighteen and deciding what to do next: stop asking "what will I learn," and start asking "what's the ROI on this specific degree, from this specific school, given what it costs." That's not a cynical way to think about it. It's the accurate way. A degree is a financial instrument you buy on credit, and like any financial instrument, some versions of it are a great trade and others will follow you around for twenty years while paying for themselves never.

The market for this instrument is badly distorted. There are too many providers charging high fees for degrees that don't clear a reasonable bar for return on investment, and the whole thing keeps running because government subsidies and loan guarantees remove the market discipline that would otherwise kill off the weak providers. If a bank had to underwrite every student loan on the actual expected earnings of the degree being financed, half of the higher education industry would not survive the year.

## Two very different products, one label

Zoom out and the market sorts itself into two tiers wearing the same name. The most selective handful of schools sit on multibillion-dollar endowments, hand their graduates a straight line into the most prestigious companies on earth, and double as, frankly, an elite mating market — you're not just choosing classmates, you're choosing a social class. Everyone else is selling a considerably worse investment, at a similar or higher price, propped up by the same subsidies, and increasingly by a pipeline of international students who provide the tuition revenue that keeps the doors open.

Both tiers get called "college." Both get subsidized by the same government programs. Only one of them is actually worth what it costs. Know which one you're buying before you sign the loan paperwork — because the institution selling it to you has no incentive to tell you the difference.
